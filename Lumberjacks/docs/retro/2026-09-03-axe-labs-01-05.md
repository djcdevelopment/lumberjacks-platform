# Retrospective: Axe Labs 01–05

**Dates:** 2026-09-02 through 2026-09-03

**Scope:** articulated swing, chopping head, contact witness, one stateful bite, and opposing stroke

**Human acceptance:** DOWN/top stroke on 2026-09-02; UP/under stroke and Lab 05 surface on 2026-09-03

## What changed

The work began with a playable Lumberjacks build whose trees were improving but whose player,
camera, and chopping interaction were still visibly prototype-grade. The immediate temptation was to
keep integrating the forest, network, persistence, and tree-felling systems. Instead, the axe was
removed from the game loop and rebuilt as a chain of small laboratories.

The result is five independently runnable stages:

| Lab | Added | Explicitly excluded |
|---|---|---|
| 01 | Articulated shoulder/elbow/wrist arc and timing | Axe head, target, wood, damage, network |
| 02 | Physical handle, chopping head, and cutting edge | Contact and material response |
| 03 | Finite neutral witness, arrival vectors, exact pause, follow-through | Force, wood, damage |
| 04 | Uniform trunk, energy/aim penetration, kerf, cut mark, rings, retention | Accumulation, chip release, fall |
| 05 | Accepted opposing UP/DOWN strokes, two lips, centered tangent contact | Wood, retention, chip, damage, network |

The earlier all-in-one `TreeFellingLab` remains useful reference material for polar trunk state,
failure ideas, and compact projection. It is no longer evidence that the axe interaction itself is
accepted.

## Evidence at closure

- `accepted-axe-v1` pins the human-reviewed downward/top stroke.
- `accepted-axe-up-v1` pins the human-reviewed upward/under stroke.
- 186 deterministic Game.Contracts tests pass.
- The Godot C# client builds with zero warnings and zero errors.
- Lab 05 starts with Vulkan and D3D12; Labs 01–04 received regression startup checks after the shared
  actor-root change.
- Lab 05 was played and visually accepted by Derek on OMEN.
- No live server, multiplayer authority, persistence, chip release, or falling claim is made.

## Decisions that paid off

### One lab, one human question

The strongest move was refusing to make “axe chopping” one task. A person could identify an upside-
down texture, wrong rotation axis, locked elbow, implausible shoulder sweep, or firewood-splitting arc
in seconds. An agent could otherwise spend hours tuning equations around the mistake. Each lab made
the error legible before another variable arrived.

### Freeze accepted values in executable tests

Visual acceptance became named code: `accepted-axe-v1` and `accepted-axe-up-v1`. Tests pin geometry,
timing, joint keyframes, opposing vertical direction, target registration, and frame-rate-independent
pausing. “Accepted” is no longer a conversational memory.

### Preserve follow-through

The axe does not stop conceptually at the bark. Aim-through and follow-through remain distinct from
actual penetration. Lab 03 shows the committed free path; Lab 04 lets material resistance stop the
head. This keeps player intent separate from the resulting bite.

### Model retention as an event

An embedded axe is not an animation delay. It changes the interaction state: no backswing exists
until the player frees it with `F`. A free head withdraws and recovers automatically. That small rule
makes the tool feel materially connected to the wood.

### Keep both raster state and exact cut history

The 5 mm field answers “which material is kerf or released?” Retained polylines answer “where did the
edge travel?” Keeping both supports efficient resistance lookup without losing the intersections
needed to construct a later chip.

### Make the input embody the stroke

Left mouse for the under-swing and right mouse for the top-swing felt more natural than a mode toggle
followed by a generic attack. The on-screen switch remains useful for inspection, but the primary
control now communicates the physical choice directly.

### Permit honest presentation cheats

Whole-stance target registration made the placeholder capsule cross the ground plane. Rather than
distort contact, the character became a slime/ooze: it squashes wider or stretches narrower while
preserving scaled volume and staying grounded. Later, its green shell grew 20 percent and moved 6 cm
back for a better human-to-tree silhouette. The measured skeleton and tool remain exact.

### Fix the reference frame before adding material

The first Lab 05 trunk was far too small relative to the head, and the cutting edge met it corner-
first. Increasing the witness to 1.5 m diameter and making the edge tangent at centered contact
immediately clarified leverage, bite width, and future binding risk. Scale and orientation were part
of the experiment, not cosmetic cleanup.

## Friction and mistakes

### Integration was attempted before the interaction was understood

The project already had networking, persistence, deployment automation, forest rendering, and an
older felling prototype. That made integration look like the shortest route. In practice, a weak
swing contaminated every downstream observation. The hard part was not joining working vertical
slices; it was discovering that one of the slices had never passed a human-motion gate.

### Early visual rigs moved the wrong amount—or only one joint

The first articulated revision effectively swung the shoulder while downstream joints remained
locked. Later versions needed substantially more shoulder rotation and explicit elbow/wrist leverage.
The bug was obvious in motion and under-specified in prose.

### “Axe swing” hid two different motions

An early vertical arc looked like splitting firewood, not felling a standing tree. The horizontal
fell plane fixed the first stroke, but notch construction then exposed a second requirement: a real
upward under-the-shoulder motion. Mirroring the future cut math would have hidden the missing action.

### Names drifted ahead of meaning

“Cut width” was briefly described as axial width even though it represented a simplified tangential
face band. Correcting the name before accumulation prevented the wrong dimension from becoming a
protocol assumption. Effective curved-bark contact width remains deliberately unresolved.

### The target could make bad physics look acceptable

A small cylinder and an oblique edge made the original contact difficult to read. A solver can make
the cutting center hit almost anything by translating it. Requiring the whole stance to move,
requiring actual vertical direction, enlarging the trunk, and reporting tangent alignment made the
remaining compromises visible.

### The lab UI occasionally competed with the experiment

Text initially overlapped the swing, the window needed more vertical space, and important state lived
away from the comparison view. Maximizing the lab and moving the sense toggle and CAD views to the
right improved review. The lesson is simple: diagnostic layout is part of test quality.

## What got lucky

- The rig already decomposed naturally into shoulder, elbow, hand, handle, and head nodes.
- C# partial classes let each lab add a surface without forking the base scene.
- Existing pure kinematics made velocity, exact contact, and renderer-independent tests cheap.
- The tuning panel made human correction faster than rebuilding after every visual observation.
- The old polar model had already established that rich lab state and compact network state can be
  separate, even though its swing animation was unsuitable.

## Patterns worth keeping

1. **Ask for a visible yes/no before adding the next variable.** Numerical correctness and physical
   feel are separate gates.
2. **Turn acceptance into an immutable named calibration.** Reset means “return to accepted,” not
   “return to whatever defaults happen to be in the scene.”
3. **Use pure state beneath a disposable projection.** Godot can change how evidence is shown without
   changing the deterministic result.
4. **Let agents build the measurement surface; let the human notice the absurdity.** The collaboration
   is strongest when neither side pretends to have the other's sensorium.
5. **Keep state transitions literal.** Embedded means no recovery. Same-direction cuts mean no chip.
   Contact means an exact sample, not a vague animation moment.
6. **Treat proportions and controls as mechanics.** Tree scale, edge orientation, and mouse mapping
   changed understanding of the system, not merely appearance.
7. **Stop at acceptance boundaries.** Creative attention is finite. A written, tested checkpoint is
   more valuable than starting the next coupled system while the reviewer is depleted.

## What Lab 06 inherits

Lab 06 can now assume two accepted motions and should add only accumulated material history:

- one trunk retains every strike until `R` supplies fresh wood;
- left mouse remains UP and right mouse remains DOWN;
- old cuts are muted, the newest is bright, and intersections are explicit;
- same-direction strikes can deepen or widen but cannot produce a chip;
- effective blade width is clipped against curved bark rather than treated as the full 34 cm edge or
  the temporary 10 cm band;
- opposing cuts may define a translucent potential-chip volume with depth, width, area, and volume;
- chip release, fracture, falling, damage, authority, persistence, and networking remain off.

The next success criterion is not “a tree falls.” It is “the retained cuts make the candidate chip
mathematically and visually inevitable.”
